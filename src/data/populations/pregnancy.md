## The short answer

Stopping smoking is one of the most useful things you can do in pregnancy, and it helps at any stage. The [quit-smoking guide](/quit-smoking/) covers the general case; this page covers what changes in pregnancy. The first-line treatment is not a drug: it is [behavioural support](/methods/choosing-a-method/) — counselling, a stop-smoking service, a quitline, text support or vouchers for staying smoke-free. That support works in pregnancy, and it carries no medication exposure for the baby.

Medication is a second-line conversation. UK guidance allows nicotine replacement therapy to be considered at the earliest opportunity, under supervision, because the risks of licensed NRT are much lower than the risks of continued smoking. US guidance is more cautious and finds the evidence insufficient to assess pharmacotherapy in pregnancy. Both positions can be true at once: NRT may help some people, but the evidence is not strong enough to make it a universal recommendation, and the decision belongs with a clinician who knows your pregnancy.

Varenicline, bupropion and cytisine should not be used in pregnancy or breastfeeding. E-cigarettes are not recommended as a cessation aid in pregnancy; the safety data are not there.

## What smoking does in pregnancy

Smoking during pregnancy is associated with a long list of complications: miscarriage, congenital anomalies, stillbirth, fetal growth restriction, preterm birth, placental abruption, and sudden infant death syndrome, as well as impaired lung function in childhood. That list comes from the evidence review behind the 2021 USPSTF recommendation, and it is the reason pregnancy guidance is more protective than general cessation advice.

The harm is not only nicotine. Tobacco smoke contains carbon monoxide, tar and thousands of other chemicals, and several of them affect the placenta and fetal oxygen supply. NICE makes this point explicitly when discussing NRT: most smoking-related health problems are caused by components of tobacco smoke other than nicotine, and the risks from licensed nicotine products are much lower than those from smoking. That does not make NRT risk-free, but it explains why "nicotine" and "smoking" are not interchangeable in this conversation.

Secondhand smoke matters too. A smoke-free home and car reduce exposure for you and for the baby after birth. NICE recommends that household members who smoke are offered help to stop, not simply asked to smoke outside.

## Why quitting at any stage matters

The earliest quit gives the largest benefit, because it avoids more of the exposure during the weeks when the baby's organs and growth are most vulnerable. But the message is not "too late". Quitting later in pregnancy still reduces the risks compared with continuing, and quitting before birth improves the environment the baby is born into.

NICE recommends carbon monoxide testing at the first antenatal appointment and again at 36 weeks, with further testing at other appointments for people who smoke, are quitting, used to smoke, or had a reading of 4 parts per million or above at booking. The test is not a test of honesty; it is a physical measure that shows how much carbon monoxide is in your blood, and it gives you and your midwife a baseline to compare against.

If you are pregnant and smoking, the offer of support should come to you. NICE recommends an opt-out referral to stop-smoking support for anyone who reports smoking, has stopped in the past two weeks, or has a carbon monoxide reading at or above the threshold. You can decline, but you should not have to hunt for the service.

## Behavioural support: the first-line treatment

The evidence for behavioural support in pregnancy is stronger than the evidence for medication. A Cochrane review published in 2017 included 102 trials, with 88 trials and more than 28,000 women providing data on abstinence in late pregnancy. Counselling increased cessation compared with usual care (risk ratio 1.44, 95% CI 1.19 to 1.73; 30 studies; high-quality evidence) and compared with less intensive interventions (RR 1.25, 1.07 to 1.47; 18 studies). The effect persisted after birth: abstinence at zero to five months postpartum (RR 1.59, 1.26 to 2.01; 11 studies) and at 12 to 17 months (RR 2.20, 1.23 to 3.96; two studies).

Incentive schemes also have evidence. The same review found that incentive-based interventions increased cessation compared with a non-contingent incentive (RR 2.36, 1.36 to 4.09; four studies), and NICE recommends voucher incentives for pregnant people who stop, with abstinence confirmed by a carbon monoxide reading below 4 parts per million. NICE cites incentives totalling around £400 as effective. The point of the voucher is not to bribe you; it is to make the effort visible and to reward the behaviour that is hardest in the first weeks.

What support actually looks like varies. It can be a weekly session with a stop-smoking adviser, a telephone quitline, a text programme, or a midwife-led conversation with follow-up. NICE recommends intensive and ongoing support rather than a single brief intervention, with carbon monoxide monitoring used as encouragement and feedback. If the first service does not fit, ask for another. The evidence is for support that continues, not for one appointment.

## Nicotine replacement therapy: the honest position

This is where guidance differs, and the difference is worth understanding rather than averaging away.

USPSTF recommends behavioural interventions for pregnant people who smoke (grade A) and concludes that the current evidence is insufficient to assess the balance of benefits and harms of pharmacotherapy (I statement). An I statement is not a prohibition. It means the evidence does not let a guideline make a population-level recommendation either way.

NICE takes a more permissive position. It recommends considering [NRT](/medications/nicotine-patch/) at the earliest opportunity in pregnancy, continuing it after pregnancy if needed to prevent relapse, and explaining that the risks of licensed NRT are much lower than those of smoking. It also recommends that patches are removed before going to bed. Varenicline, bupropion and cytisine should not be offered in pregnancy or breastfeeding.

The Cochrane review behind the medication question included 11 trials and 2,412 pregnant women who smoked at enrolment: nine trials of NRT and two of bupropion, all with behavioural support in both arms. For NRT versus control, the review found low-certainty evidence of increased abstinence in later pregnancy (RR 1.37, 95% CI 1.08 to 1.74; nine studies; 2,336 women). The result was not consistent across trial designs: in placebo-controlled trials the effect was uncertain (RR 1.21, 0.95 to 1.55; six studies), while non-placebo-controlled trials showed a larger benefit (RR 8.55, 2.05 to 35.71; three studies). The review found no evidence of a difference between NRT and control in miscarriage, stillbirth, preterm birth, birthweight, low birthweight, neonatal intensive care admission, caesarean section, congenital abnormalities or neonatal death. Adherence to NRT was generally low.

That is a nuanced picture, and it should be presented as one. NRT may help some pregnant people quit, particularly when behavioural support alone has not worked. The evidence is not strong enough to promise that it will, and the placebo-controlled data are compatible with little or no average effect. The safety data are reassuring for the outcomes measured, but they are not a guarantee of long-term safety. This is exactly the kind of decision that belongs with a clinician who can weigh your pregnancy, your smoking history and your preferences.

What the page will not do is give you a dose or a schedule. NRT in pregnancy is prescribed or supplied with instructions, and the right dose depends on how much you smoke, how you use the product and how you respond. Follow the prescriber and the label; do not combine products or increase the dose on your own. The [medication pages](/medications/) explain the class-level differences, and [can I use NRT while pregnant?](/questions/) answers the question directly.

## E-cigarettes

E-cigarettes are not recommended as a cessation aid in pregnancy. USPSTF finds the evidence insufficient to assess their benefits and harms for cessation in adults, including pregnant people, and recommends directing people to interventions with proven effectiveness and established safety. NICE notes that nicotine-containing e-cigarettes are not licensed medicines in the UK and that there is not enough evidence to know the long-term harms. The safety of e-cigarettes in pregnancy is not established.

If you are using e-cigarettes and are pregnant, tell your maternity team. The conversation should be about switching to a licensed option or stopping nicotine altogether, not about blame.

## Withdrawal in pregnancy

Nicotine withdrawal is uncomfortable but not medically dangerous to the baby. The common symptoms — craving, irritability, low mood, restlessness, poor sleep, increased appetite and difficulty concentrating — are the same ones non-pregnant people experience, and most settle over two to four weeks. The [withdrawal timeline](/withdrawal/timeline/) maps the usual course. Pregnancy adds its own symptoms: nausea, fatigue, heartburn, mood swings and disturbed sleep can all overlap with withdrawal, which makes the first weeks harder to read.

Do not treat withdrawal as a reason to resume smoking. If symptoms are severe, ask for help rather than waiting for them to pass. And do not attribute every symptom to withdrawal: contact your maternity team for bleeding, severe abdominal pain, reduced fetal movements, severe headache or visual changes, or anything else that worries you.

## After birth and breastfeeding

NICE recommends continuing NRT after pregnancy if it is needed to prevent relapse, including if the pregnancy does not continue, and says varenicline, bupropion and cytisine should not be offered during breastfeeding. That leaves NRT as the medication option to discuss with a clinician, alongside behavioural support. The decision depends on your breastfeeding situation and your history; it is not a self-service choice.

The postpartum period is a high-risk time for returning to smoking. Sleep deprivation, stress, changed routines and the end of pregnancy-specific support all pull in the same direction. Plan for it before the baby arrives: who will help, what the smoke-free rules are, and what you will do when someone offers you a cigarette. The [relapse prevention section](/relapse/) is worth reading before you need it. A smoke-free home also protects the baby from secondhand smoke, which is associated with respiratory illness and sudden infant death syndrome.

## If you cannot quit yet

Not everyone can stop on the first attempt, and shame does not help anyone quit. If you are not ready or have relapsed, stay in contact with your maternity team and the stop-smoking service; [can I quit while pregnant?](/questions/) sets out the short answer. NICE supports harm-reduction approaches for people who do not want or are not ready to stop in one go, while keeping the offer of a full quit open. In pregnancy, the priority remains stopping, but reducing the number of cigarettes and eliminating smoke from the home are still worth doing.

The most useful question is not "why can't you quit?" but "what would make the next attempt more likely to work?" More support, a different support format, treatment for nausea or sleep, help with childcare, or a medicine review can all change the answer.

## Bottom line

Stopping smoking in pregnancy is worth doing at any stage, and behavioural support is the first-line treatment with the strongest evidence. NRT is an option under supervision, not a guaranteed fix; the evidence is low certainty and the guidance differs between countries. Varenicline, bupropion and cytisine should not be used, and e-cigarettes are not recommended. The [medications section](/medications/) explains why the options differ, and the [special situations hub](/populations/) covers other groups. Plan the support, make the home smoke-free, and keep the conversation with your maternity team open.
