## The short answer

If you live with depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia or another mental health condition, the evidence does not ask you to choose between your mental health and stopping smoking. It points the other way: on average, people who stop smoking report lower depression, anxiety and stress afterwards than people who continue.

That average hides a difficult opening. The first weeks of withdrawal can include low mood, irritability, poor sleep and anxiety, and those symptoms can feel like a relapse of the condition you already manage. The difference between a manageable withdrawal dip and something that needs clinical attention is worth planning for before the quit date, not after it.

This page covers what the research shows, why psychiatric medication needs a review around the quit date, and how to build support that fits. It is general education, not a personal treatment plan, and it does not replace your mental health team.

## Why smoking and mental health are tangled

Smoking is more common among people with mental health conditions than in the general population, and dependence is often stronger. There is no single reason. Nicotine can briefly relieve stress and low mood, which makes it easy to experience smoking as self-treatment. Smoking also structures the day: it provides a break, a reason to leave a difficult room, and a social routine. For someone whose life is already organised around managing symptoms, removing it can feel like removing a support.

There is also a history of clinical caution that has outlived the evidence. For years, quitting was treated as a risk to mental stability, and some people were advised to delay it. That advice was based more on concern than on trial data. The evidence now available shows that, on average, quitting is followed by better mental health, not worse, and that people with psychiatric conditions can use the same cessation treatments as everyone else, with monitoring.

The practical differences are real, though. Withdrawal symptoms overlap with psychiatric symptoms, so a change in mood is harder to interpret. Some psychiatric medicines are affected by smoking, so stopping can change their blood levels. And the support has to be delivered by someone who takes both histories seriously. That is a reason to plan more carefully, not a reason to keep smoking.

## What the evidence says about quitting and mood

The strongest summary is a systematic review and meta-analysis published in the BMJ in 2014; the short answer to [does quitting improve mental health?](/questions/) draws on the same evidence. It pooled 26 longitudinal studies that measured mental health before and after quitting, with follow-up from seven weeks to nine years. Compared with people who continued to smoke, people who quit had lower anxiety, depression, mixed anxiety and depression, and stress, and better positive mood and psychological quality of life.

The effect sizes were moderate, not trivial. The standardised mean difference for anxiety was −0.37 (95% confidence interval −0.70 to −0.03), for depression −0.25 (−0.37 to −0.12), for mixed anxiety and depression −0.31 (−0.47 to −0.14), and for stress −0.27 (−0.40 to −0.13). Positive affect rose by 0.40 (0.09 to 0.71) and psychological quality of life by 0.22 (0.09 to 0.36). The authors reported no evidence that the effect size differed between the general population and populations with physical or psychiatric disorders, and concluded that the effects were equal to or larger than those of antidepressant treatment for mood and anxiety disorders.

That last sentence needs care. It is a comparison of effect sizes across different literatures, not a claim that quitting treats clinical depression or replaces medication. What it does support is the central point: the feared worsening of mental health after quitting is not what the average evidence shows.

The exception is the short term. A separate review of tobacco abstinence symptoms, summarised in the [withdrawal timeline](/withdrawal/timeline/), found that depressed mood, irritability, anxiety, restlessness, poor concentration, insomnia and increased appetite are valid withdrawal symptoms, and that most peak in the first week and settle over two to four weeks. Craving and increased appetite can last longer. So the first weeks can genuinely feel worse while the longer trajectory is better. Both things are true, and knowing that makes the dip easier to interpret.

## The first weeks: withdrawal or something more?

The first week is usually the loudest. Mood can drop, patience shortens, sleep breaks up, and concentration becomes unreliable. If you have an anxiety disorder, the physical restlessness of withdrawal can feel like anxiety; if you have depression, the low mood of withdrawal can feel like a downturn. That overlap is normal, and it is why a before-and-after plan matters more than a diagnosis made in the middle of a bad afternoon.

The useful question is not "is this withdrawal?" but "is this the pattern I expected, and is it changing?" Withdrawal symptoms tend to peak and then ease. A mood change that deepens, persists beyond the first few weeks, or brings hopelessness, self-harm thoughts, panic or a loss of function is not something to wait out alone.

| What is commonly part of early withdrawal | What needs professional advice |
| --- | --- |
| Low mood or irritability that comes in waves and starts to ease after the first week | Low mood that deepens, persists for weeks, or makes it hard to get through the day |
| Broken sleep, vivid dreams, tiredness | Severe insomnia that is still worsening after the first month |
| Restlessness, a racing feeling, difficulty settling | Panic attacks, severe anxiety, or fear of leaving the house |
| Poor concentration and forgetfulness | Confusion, unusual beliefs, hearing voices, or a sense of unreality |
| Increased appetite and food cravings | Rapid weight loss, inability to eat, or other physical symptoms that worry you |

If you take psychiatric medication, add one more column: any new side effect, tremor, sedation, confusion or unusual movement should be reported to your prescriber rather than attributed to withdrawal. The medicine review described below is designed to catch that.

## Medication for quitting: what the evidence supports

Major guidelines do not carve out a different first-line treatment for people with mental health conditions. The [medications section](/medications/) covers the full comparison; in short, WHO's 2024 guideline recommends combining behavioural support with pharmacotherapy, and names [nicotine replacement therapy](/medications/nicotine-patch/), [varenicline](/medications/varenicline/), [bupropion](/medications/bupropion/) and [cytisine](/medications/cytisine/) as effective options for adults. USPSTF recommends behavioural interventions plus FDA-approved pharmacotherapy for non-pregnant adults. NICE recommends the same range, with combination NRT, varenicline and cytisine described as more likely to succeed than bupropion or a single form of NRT.

The safety question has a dedicated trial. EAGLES randomised 8,144 smokers, including 4,116 with psychiatric disorders, to varenicline, bupropion, nicotine patch or placebo, with brief counselling at every visit. The primary outcome was moderate or severe neuropsychiatric adverse events. In the psychiatric cohort, those events occurred in 6.5% of the varenicline group, 6.7% of the bupropion group, 5.2% of the patch group and 4.9% of the placebo group. The risk differences versus placebo were 1.59 percentage points (95% CI −0.42 to 3.59) for varenicline and 1.78 (−0.24 to 3.81) for bupropion — not statistically significant. In the non-psychiatric cohort, the rates were 1.3% with varenicline, 2.2% with bupropion, 2.5% with patch and 2.4% with placebo.

EAGLES also measured quitting. Varenicline produced higher abstinence than placebo (odds ratio 3.61, 95% CI 3.07 to 4.24), nicotine patch (1.68, 1.46 to 1.93) and bupropion (1.75, 1.52 to 2.01). Bupropion and patch both beat placebo. Efficacy did not differ by cohort. The trial's conclusion was that it did not show a significant increase in neuropsychiatric adverse events attributable to varenicline or bupropion relative to patch or placebo.

Two limits matter. First, a trial average is not a personal prediction; some people will have a difficult reaction, and that should be reported. Second, EAGLES was not designed to cover every severe or unstable presentation, and it did not settle every question about long-term psychiatric safety. The reasonable reading is that these medicines are usable in this group with ordinary clinical monitoring, not that they are risk-free.

NRT has the advantage of fewer drug interactions, which is why it is often the simplest option when someone takes several psychiatric medicines. Varenicline and bupropion require a prescriber's review. Cytisine is available in some countries but not others. The choice is a conversation about your history, your medicines and your preferences — not a hierarchy you have to guess at. The [method decision guide](/methods/choosing-a-method/) sets out the questions to bring to that conversation.

## The interaction that is easy to miss

Tobacco smoke changes how the body handles some prescribed medicines. When smoking stops, those medicines can be cleared more slowly, so blood levels rise and side effects can appear. NICE names clozapine, olanzapine, theophylline and warfarin as medicines affected by smoking or stopping smoking, and recommends monitoring efficacy and adverse effects and adjusting the dose as appropriate.

For clozapine in particular, this is not a detail to leave until after the quit date. A rise in blood level can produce sedation, drooling, constipation, dizziness or more serious effects, and the safe response is a planned review rather than an emergency one. The same principle applies more broadly: ask your prescriber whether any of your medicines need monitoring or a dose review when you stop.

A practical script helps. Before the quit date, say: "I am stopping smoking on this date. Are any of my medicines affected by that, and what is the plan for monitoring or adjusting them?" If you cannot reach the prescriber, ask a pharmacist to check the list. Do not change a psychiatric medicine on your own, and do not stop it because you are worried about interactions — that is the more dangerous move.

## Behavioural support that fits

Medication works better with support, and the support has to fit the person. NICE recommends that people with severe mental health conditions who may need additional help are offered a specialist adviser with mental health expertise, and support whose duration and intensity are tailored to their needs. That is a useful standard to ask for, even where services are thin.

In practice, support can come from several places at once: a stop-smoking service, a therapist or key worker who already knows your history, a telephone quitline, a peer group, or a digital programme. The common ingredient is continuity — someone who checks in after the first bad week, not only on the quit date. If you are under the care of a mental health team, tell them the quit date. They can help distinguish withdrawal from a change in your condition, and they can coordinate the medicine review.

If you are admitted to hospital or use inpatient mental health services, ask how nicotine withdrawal will be managed. NICE expects mental health services to provide stop-smoking support and to offer licensed nicotine-containing products for temporary abstinence where needed. You should not have to manage withdrawal unsupported because the building is smoke-free.

## A practical plan

The plan below is a template, not a prescription. Its purpose is to put the medicine review, the support and the mood monitoring in the right order. It follows the same sequence as the [first 72 hours](/first-72-hours/) and [first month](/first-month/) guides.

| Stage | What to arrange | Who |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Two to four weeks before the quit date | Review every psychiatric medicine for interactions and monitoring; choose a cessation medicine; book behavioural support; tell your therapist or key worker | Prescriber, pharmacist, stop-smoking adviser |
| Quit date | Stop smoking; start the cessation medicine exactly as supplied; remove cigarettes, lighters and ashtrays; plan the first trigger times | You, with the adviser |
| First week | Keep support contact; expect low mood, irritability and sleep disruption; report new medicine side effects; use the craving plan | Adviser, prescriber if needed |
| Weeks two to four | Review mood and sleep; do not stop the cessation medicine early; adjust support if the plan is not working | Adviser, prescriber |
| Months two to three | Build relapse prevention around triggers; review whether support should continue; check in on mood beyond withdrawal | You, adviser, mental health team |

The plan should include one named person you can contact when a craving or a mood dip is winning. "Call someone" is not a plan; "call this number at this time" is.

## If you slip

A single cigarette is not a failed quit. The risk is the routine that can rebuild around it: one becomes one a day, and one a day becomes the old pattern. The useful response is to treat it as information. What was the trigger? Was the medication being used as supplied? Had support stopped? Was there a mood change that needs attention?

Do not stop the cessation medicine because of one slip unless a clinician tells you to. Do not skip the next support contact because you feel ashamed. The [relapse and recovery section](/relapse/) and the [cravings guide](/cravings/) set out how to reset without turning one cigarette into a routine. If the slip followed a significant mood change, address that first with your mental health team.

## What not to do

- Do not stop or reduce psychiatric medication to "do the quit properly". That is the change most likely to cause harm.
- Do not assume that quitting will worsen your mental health and delay indefinitely. The average evidence points the other way.
- Do not treat every low mood in the first month as withdrawal. Persistent or severe changes need assessment.
- Do not hide the quit from your mental health team. They cannot help with a medicine review they do not know about.
- Do not rely on willpower alone if medication and support are available. Combination treatment is the better-evidenced approach.

## Bottom line

People with mental health conditions can quit, and the evidence does not support the old fear that quitting makes mental health worse on average. The first weeks can be emotionally difficult, and the medicine review is not optional. Plan the support, review the prescriptions, monitor the mood, and treat a slip as a problem to solve rather than a verdict. The goal is not a perfect quit; it is a quit that survives a bad week. If you are still deciding how to start, the [method decision guide](/methods/choosing-a-method/) is the next step.
