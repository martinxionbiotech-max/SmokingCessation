Combination nicotine replacement means using two forms at once: a patch for a steady background level, plus gum, lozenge, inhalator or spray for cravings that arrive sharply. It is the arrangement with the best evidence in the nicotine replacement family, and also the one that asks the most planning. Whether it is worth the extra cost and fiddliness depends on the shape of your cravings and the shape of your week.

## What "combination" actually means

The word is sometimes used loosely, so it is worth being precise. Combination NRT means a slow, steady form plus a fast, on-demand form. In practice that is usually a [patch](/medications/nicotine-patch/) plus gum or a patch plus lozenge, although an inhalator or mouth spray can fill the fast-acting role. The [combination approaches page](/methods/combination/) describes how the pieces are meant to fit together.

It does not mean two patches, two gums, or a patch plus a second patch. Doubling the same form does not add coverage; it adds nicotine without adding a way to answer a spike. And it does not mean NRT plus varenicline or bupropion, which is a different kind of combination and a prescriber decision.

The logic is coverage, not strength. The patch removes the flat, grinding withdrawal that sits under the whole day. The fast-acting form answers the sudden urge tied to coffee, a break, a drink or a person. One product can do one of those jobs well. It cannot do both.

## At a glance

| Question | Combination NRT | Single-form NRT |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it covers | Background withdrawal plus breakthrough cravings | One of the two, depending on the form |
| Evidence versus single form | Higher long-term quit rates (RR 1.27) | Lower than combination |
| Effort | Two products, two techniques, more planning | One product, one technique |
| Cost | Higher | Lower |
| Side effects | Similar in kind to single-form use, possibly from two sources | Local effects from one product |
| Best fit | Both craving shapes present; previous single-form attempt stalled | One clear craving shape; simplicity or cost matters more |
| Main failure mode | Running out of the fast-acting form, or treating the patch as rescue | An uncovered craving shape |

## The evidence

Cochrane's 2023 review of NRT delivery compared combination NRT with a single form across 16 studies and 12,169 participants. Combination treatment produced higher long-term quit rates, with a risk ratio of 1.27 (95% CI 1.17 to 1.37) and high-certainty evidence. In plain terms: for every 100 quitters who would succeed on a single form, about 127 would succeed on combination, all else being equal.

That is a modest-sounding ratio with a meaningful public-health footprint, because it applies to a treatment that is already effective. The same review family establishes that any NRT roughly increases quit rates by half again to two-thirds compared with placebo or no medication. Combination is a step up from an intervention that already works.

Network meta-analyses tell a consistent story. In the Cochrane overview of pharmacological interventions, combination NRT and varenicline sit among the most effective options, ahead of single-form NRT and bupropion. The ranking is not a guarantee for an individual, but it is the best comparative map available.

| Comparison | Finding | Certainty |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Combination vs single-form NRT | RR 1.27 (1.17–1.37); 16 studies, 12,169 participants | High |
| Any NRT vs placebo or no medication | Quit rates roughly 50–70% higher | High |
| Combination NRT vs varenicline | Both among the most effective options | Moderate (indirect) |
| Single-form NRT vs nothing | Clearly effective, but lower than combination | High |

The weakness in the evidence is not about whether combination works. It is about who needs it. Most trials recruit people motivated to quit, often through health services, and report group averages. A light smoker with one predictable craving may do fine on a single form; a heavier smoker with all-day withdrawal and sharp spikes is the person the combination evidence fits best.

## Why two forms cover two different problems

Withdrawal is not one experience. There is a background version — irritability, restlessness, poor concentration, low mood, a sense that something is missing — that persists through the day. And there is a spike version — sudden, situational, intense, usually tied to a cue — that rises within minutes and fades whether or not you smoke.

The patch is built for the first. It releases nicotine slowly and keeps a level present, which is why it does not answer a craving that peaks in three minutes. Gum and lozenge are built for the second: absorbed through the mouth, fast enough to meet a spike, short enough that they can be repeated around triggers.

Most heavier smokers have both shapes. Treating only the background leaves the spikes unmanaged; treating only the spikes leaves the day-long grind unmanaged. The [cravings guide](/cravings/) explains the anatomy of an urge, the [withdrawal timeline](/withdrawal/timeline/) shows when the background component eases, and the [trigger pages](/triggers/) map the situations that produce spikes.

## Where a single form is the better choice

Combination is not automatically right. A single form can be the better first attempt when:

- **Cravings are clearly one shape.** If the problem is a constant flat withdrawal and spikes are rare, a patch alone may cover it. If the problem is a handful of predictable moments and the rest of the day is fine, gum or lozenge alone may be enough.
- **Cost is the binding constraint.** Two products cost more than one, and a course that runs out early is worse than a simpler course completed. The cheapest product you use consistently beats the most effective one you ration.
- **Simplicity protects adherence.** Some people manage one product reliably and two poorly. A single form used correctly is better than a combination used sporadically.
- **Side effects are already limiting.** If patch adhesion or gum-related jaw pain is a problem, adding a second product may not be realistic.

Single-form NRT is still a genuine treatment, not a consolation prize, and the [medications pillar](/medications/) shows where it sits against prescription options. The [NRT versus no medication comparison](/comparisons/nrt-vs-no-medication/) sets out the size of that effect, the [patch versus gum versus lozenge comparison](/comparisons/nicotine-patch-vs-gum-vs-lozenge/) helps match one form to a craving pattern, and the [three-drug comparison](/comparisons/varenicline-vs-cytisine-vs-bupropion/) shows what prescription options add.

## Matching the arrangement to your day

| If your day looks like this | Arrangement worth discussing | Practical note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| All-day withdrawal plus a rough first hour | Patch plus fast-acting form on waking | The patch cannot answer the morning spike on its own |
| Predictable spikes at meals, breaks or the drive home | Patch plus gum or lozenge timed before the trigger | Use before the craving peaks, not after |
| Unpredictable spikes in meetings or at work | Patch plus lozenge | Discreet, no chewing, no visible gum |
| Strong oral or hand habit | Patch plus gum | The gum also gives the mouth something to do |
| Skin irritation from patches | Fast-acting form alone, or rotate sites | Discuss alternatives with a pharmacist |
| Cost-constrained but both shapes present | Generic patch plus generic gum as needed | Ask a quitline or pharmacist about subsidised supply |
| Pregnant or breastfeeding | Behavioural support first; clinician decision on NRT | Do not self-combine products in pregnancy |

This is a planning table, not a prescribing table. It does not specify strengths, frequencies or tapering, because those depend on the product, the person and the clinical picture.

## Common mistakes that make combination look ineffective

**Treating the patch as rescue.** A patch takes hours to build a level. Expecting it to kill a craving in five minutes leads to the conclusion that "the patch doesn't work", when the patch was doing its actual job and the spike was never covered.

**Running out of the fast-acting form.** People often buy one pack of gum or lozenges and use it sparingly, then spend the second week white-knuckling spikes. The fast-acting half of the combination is not a garnish; it is the part that handles the moments.

**Using the gum wrong.** Chewing gum fast releases nicotine faster than the mouth can absorb it, producing hiccups, burning and nausea. The chew-and-park rhythm — a few chews, then rest against the cheek — is the technique that makes it tolerable. The [gum page](/medications/nicotine-gum/) and [lozenge page](/medications/nicotine-lozenge/) cover the detail.

**Doubling the same form.** Two patches or two gums is not combination treatment. It is the same coverage with more nicotine and more side effects.

**Smoking while using NRT.** This raises nicotine levels unpredictably and defeats the point of the attempt. The question on [whether you can smoke while using NRT](/questions/can-i-smoke-while-using-nrt/) addresses the common version of that worry.

**Abandoning after one bad day.** A bad day is data, not a verdict. The useful response is to check which shape of craving was uncovered, not to conclude that nicotine replacement failed.

## Cost, supply and adherence

Combination costs more than a single form, and the difference is front-loaded: you buy two products before you know whether the arrangement suits you. That is a real barrier, and pretending otherwise would be unhelpful.

There are ways to reduce it. Generic patches and generic gum are usually cheaper than branded versions. Quitline and national health programmes in some countries supply NRT at reduced cost or free, sometimes specifically for combination use. A pharmacist can help compare products at the same price point. Buying the fast-acting form in a quantity that matches your trigger count is more sensible than buying the smallest pack and rationing it.

Adherence is the other cost. Two products mean two routines, and the fast-acting form has to be within reach at the moment of a craving. People who keep gum in the car, the desk drawer and the kitchen are more likely to use it than people who keep it in a cupboard.

## A seven-day trial plan

| Day | Action | What to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Decide whether your cravings have one shape or two; read both labels; ask a pharmacist about your history | Is the plan based on your day, not on a ranking? |
| 2 | Start the patch routine; use the fast-acting form at the first spike | Did the spike get answered, or did you wait it out? |
| 3 | Log cravings by time, trigger and intensity | Which shape is dominant? |
| 4 | Check technique: chew-and-park, dissolve fully, avoid acidic drinks around use | Any hiccups, burning or stomach upset? |
| 5 | Review supply: do you have enough fast-acting form for the week ahead? | Will you run out before day 7? |
| 6 | Review side effects and adjust technique before changing products | Is the problem the product or the method? |
| 7 | Review with a pharmacist if anything is unmanaged; change one variable at a time | What is the next attempt's single change? |

A week is long enough to learn the technique and short enough to adjust before frustration sets in. It is not long enough to judge whether quitting will succeed; that judgement belongs to months, and to the support around the attempt rather than the product alone.

## What this page cannot tell you

It cannot tell you which products to buy, what strength to use, how often to use them, or whether combination NRT is appropriate for your medical history. Those are decisions for a pharmacist, prescriber or national quitline, made with your history in front of them. The evidence summarised here is population-level, current to the sources listed below, and does not predict any individual outcome. This page has not been reviewed by a clinician.

The practical summary is short: combination NRT beats a single form on average, the patch covers the background, the fast-acting form covers the spikes, and the arrangement only works if both halves are actually available and used. If two products are unrealistic, choose the single form that matches your dominant craving shape and use it properly — that is still an evidence-based attempt, not a failure of planning.
