Patch, gum and lozenge contain the same drug and are sold for the same purpose, which makes the choice look arbitrary. It is not. The three products differ in when nicotine arrives, how much attention they demand and which part of the craving they are good at answering. Those differences decide which one fits a given person's day.

## How each form delivers nicotine

**The patch** is transdermal. It sits on the skin and releases nicotine steadily across the day, over roughly 16 or 24 hours depending on the product. Levels rise slowly and then plateau, so the patch does not respond to anything — it simply keeps a background level present. That is its design, not a defect: it removes the constant low-grade withdrawal that erodes resolve between cigarettes.

**Gum** delivers nicotine through the lining of the mouth. It is chewed briefly and then parked against the cheek or gum, because nicotine absorbed in the stomach is both less effective and more likely to cause hiccups and nausea. Onset is a matter of minutes, which makes gum the closest thing to a rescue product among the three.

**The lozenge** also delivers through the mouth lining, but by dissolving rather than chewing. It is slower to release than gum and takes longer to finish, and it asks nothing of the jaw. For people who cannot chew, or who find gum conspicuous, it is the more practical fast-acting option.

None of the three reproduces the speed of a cigarette. That gap is why technique and expectation matter so much: a product judged against the instant hit of smoking will always feel slow.

## The three forms at a glance

| Question | Patch | Gum | Lozenge |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Delivery | Transdermal, steady | Buccal, chew and park | Buccal, dissolve |
| Onset | Slow, builds over hours | Minutes | Minutes, released over the dissolve |
| Duration | All day, then removed | Short; repeated as needed | Short to moderate |
| Technique | Apply to clean, dry skin; rotate sites | Chew a few times, then park; avoid acidic drinks around use | Let it dissolve; do not chew or swallow |
| Best at | Background withdrawal, morning levels | Sharp, predictable cravings | Sharp cravings when chewing is impractical |
| Main drawbacks | Skin irritation, vivid dreams, no spike coverage | Jaw ache, hiccups, taste, visible use | Throat irritation, taste, slower than gum |

## What the evidence says about choosing between them

Cochrane's review of nicotine replacement found that every commonly used form — patch, gum, lozenge, inhalator and spray — increases quit rates compared with placebo or no medication. The effect is substantial: roughly half again to two-thirds more people quitting, depending on the comparison.

What the same evidence base does not provide is a clean ranking of single forms against each other. Head-to-head trials exist but are fewer, and they have not established one product as clearly superior. When a class effect is shared across forms, the choice shifts from "which is strongest" to "which will this person use correctly and consistently".

The stronger finding is about combination. Network meta-analyses place combination nicotine replacement — a patch plus a fast-acting form — among the most effective cessation options, above single-form use. That result is consistent across analyses and is the basis for the guideline advice to offer combination treatment where it is acceptable and affordable.

So the practical hierarchy is: any nicotine replacement beats none; combination beats a single form; and within single forms, fit and technique decide more than the label does.

## The distinction that matters more than the product

The useful question is not "which product is best" but "what shape are your cravings".

Background withdrawal is the flat, grinding version: irritability, restlessness, difficulty concentrating, a sense that something is missing that does not go away between cigarettes. It is present much of the day and is what the patch is built for.

Breakthrough cravings are the spike version: sudden, situational, often tied to a cue — the first coffee, a work break, the drive home, alcohol, a person. They arrive fast, peak within minutes and fade whether or not you smoke. That shape is what gum and lozenge are built for.

Most heavier smokers have both. Treating only one shape leaves the other unmanaged, which is why single-form attempts often fail for reasons that have nothing to do with motivation. The [cravings section](/cravings/) covers the anatomy of an urge, and the [trigger pages](/triggers/) map the situations that produce spikes.

## Matching the form to the pattern

| If your day looks like this | Start with | Why | Caveat |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Constant background withdrawal, low mood, poor concentration | Patch | Steady levels across the day | Will not answer a sudden spike; consider adding a fast-acting form |
| Sharp cravings at fixed moments (morning, meals, breaks) | Gum | Fast onset; can be timed to the moment | Technique matters; jaw side effects are common |
| Unpredictable cravings in meetings or at work | Lozenge | Discreet, no chewing, no visible gum | Slower than gum; throat irritation possible |
| Heavy smoking with both background and spikes | Patch plus gum or lozenge | Covers both shapes at once | More cost and complexity; confirm with a pharmacist |
| Sensitive skin or patch adhesion problems | Gum or lozenge | Avoids transdermal application | Oral side effects replace skin effects |
| Jaw problems, dentures or facial pain | Patch or lozenge | Avoids chewing | Lozenge still requires a dissolving technique |

## Technique decides whether it works

The most common reason a product "did not work" is that it was used in a way that prevents absorption.

Gum is chewed too fast by most first-time users, which releases nicotine faster than the mouth can absorb it; the result is hiccups, burning and nausea, and the person concludes the gum disagrees with them. The chew-and-park rhythm — a few chews, then rest against the cheek, repeat — solves most of it. Acidic drinks such as coffee, juice and soda reduce absorption, so timing matters around use. The [nicotine gum page](/medications/nicotine-gum/) sets out the technique in more detail.

Lozenges are often chewed or swallowed out of habit. Both reduce the effect and increase stomach upset. They need to dissolve slowly, and eating or drinking immediately around use interferes with absorption. The [lozenge page](/medications/nicotine-lozenge/) covers the practical points.

Patches fail for different reasons: applied to damp or hairy skin, left on too long, or expected to answer a craving within minutes. Rotating the application site reduces irritation, and removing the patch overnight is a common response to vivid dreams — a decision to make with the product label or a pharmacist. The [patch page](/medications/nicotine-patch/) has the detail.

One rule applies to all three: do not smoke while using nicotine replacement. The combination raises nicotine levels unpredictably and undermines the point of the attempt. The question on [whether you can smoke while using NRT](/questions/can-i-smoke-while-using-nrt/) answers the common version of that worry.

## Side effects and who should ask first

The side effects are local and usually manageable. Patch: skin irritation at the site, vivid dreams or disturbed sleep, occasionally headache. Gum: jaw ache, mouth soreness, hiccups, stomach upset. Lozenge: throat or mouth irritation, hiccups, heartburn, nausea. Most settle with correct technique, and a switch to another form is a reasonable response if they do not.

Three groups should speak to a pharmacist or clinician before starting. People with a recent heart attack, stroke or unstable angina, because nicotine affects the cardiovascular system and their treatment plan matters. People who are pregnant or breastfeeding, because behavioural support is the recommended first line and any nicotine use should be under professional guidance. And people with temporomandibular joint problems or significant dental work, who may want to avoid gum.

Nicotine replacement is not risk-free, but the comparison that matters is with continued smoking, which is substantially more harmful. That framing comes from the public health guidance, not from this site, and it is the reason NRT is available without prescription in many countries.

## Combining forms

Combination treatment means a patch for the background plus gum or lozenge for spikes. It is the arrangement with the strongest evidence, and it is what the network meta-analyses rank among the most effective options.

It is also the arrangement that requires the most planning: two products, two techniques, two cost lines. The [combination NRT comparison](/comparisons/combination-nrt-vs-single-form/) sets out the trade-off, and the [combination approaches page](/methods/combination/) describes how the pieces fit together. Anyone considering it should confirm the arrangement with a pharmacist rather than stacking products by guesswork.

## Cost and access

All three are available without prescription in many countries, which makes them the most accessible pharmacological option. The patch is usually the cheapest per day because one purchase covers a day; gum and lozenge are used repeatedly and run out faster. Generic versions are typically cheaper than branded ones, and quitline or health-service programmes sometimes supply NRT at reduced cost or free.

Cost is a real constraint and worth planning before the quit date rather than discovering on day three. The cheapest product you will use consistently is a better investment than the most effective one you ration.

## If one form did not work

Before concluding that nicotine replacement does not work for you, check three things: was the technique right, was the dose adequate for your level of dependence (a question for a pharmacist, not this page), and was the form matched to your craving pattern.

If the answer is yes to all three and the attempt still failed, the next step is usually not another single form but a different arrangement: combine a patch with a fast-acting product, add behavioural support, or discuss prescription options. The [medications section](/medications/) compares the classes, and the [comparison against no medication](/comparisons/nrt-vs-no-medication/) puts the size of the effect in context. Behavioural support is worth adding at any stage — the [counselling page](/methods/counseling/) explains what it involves.

## A seven-day trial plan

| Day | Action | What to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Pick the form that matches your craving pattern; read the label; ask a pharmacist about your history | Is the choice based on your day, not on a ranking? |
| 2 | Use it at the first craving rather than waiting for it to peak | How long until relief? |
| 3 | Log cravings by time, trigger and intensity | Are they background, situational, or both? |
| 4 | Check technique: chew-and-park, dissolve fully, avoid acidic drinks around use | Any hiccups, burning or stomach upset? |
| 5 | If background withdrawal dominates, discuss adding a patch; if spikes dominate, discuss adding a fast-acting form | Is one shape still unmanaged? |
| 6 | Review side effects and adjust technique before abandoning the product | Is the problem the product or the method? |
| 7 | Review with a pharmacist if anything still feels unmanaged; change one variable | What is the next attempt's single change? |

A week is long enough to learn the technique and short enough to adjust before frustration sets in. It is not long enough to judge whether quitting will succeed — that judgement belongs to months, not days, and to the support around the attempt rather than the product alone.

## What this page cannot tell you

It cannot tell you which product to buy, what dose to use, or whether nicotine replacement is appropriate for your medical history. Those are decisions for a pharmacist, prescriber or national quitline, made with your history in front of them. The evidence summarised here is population-level, current to the sources listed below, and does not predict any individual outcome.
