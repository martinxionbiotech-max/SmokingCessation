E-cigarettes and nicotine replacement are both ways of giving nicotine without burning tobacco. That is roughly where the similarity ends. One is a licensed medicine with a standardised dose and decades of use behind it; the other is a consumer product whose contents vary, whose long-term effects are still being studied, and which major health bodies treat very differently.

The trial evidence is more favourable to e-cigarettes than most people expect. The guideline evidence is more divided than the trial evidence suggests. Both statements are true at once, and any honest page has to hold them together.

## At a glance

| Question | Nicotine e-cigarettes | Licensed NRT |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it is | A device that heats a nicotine liquid into an aerosol | Patch, gum, lozenge, inhalator or mouth spray |
| Legal status | Consumer product in most countries | Licensed medicine in many countries |
| Quit rates vs the other | Higher in pooled trials (RR 1.55) | Lower in the same pooled trials |
| Product consistency | Variable; nicotine content may not match the label | Standardised |
| Behavioural fit | Replaces the hand-to-mouth ritual and the throat hit | Replaces nicotine; does not reproduce the ritual |
| Common side effects | Throat and mouth irritation, headache, cough, nausea | Skin irritation, hiccups, jaw ache, throat irritation |
| Long-term safety | Unknown | Well studied |
| Guideline position | Allowed in UK guidance; not recommended by WHO at population level | Recommended first-line by WHO, USPSTF and NICE |
| Main risk in practice | Dual use with cigarettes | Under-use and poor technique |

## What the trials found

Cochrane's living review is the most comprehensive synthesis available, and its 2025 update pooled 104 studies and 30,366 participants. The headline comparison is against NRT: nicotine e-cigarettes produced higher quit rates, with a risk ratio of 1.55 (95% CI 1.28 to 1.88), high-certainty evidence, across nine studies and 2,703 participants. In absolute terms, that translates to roughly three additional quitters per 100 people.

| Comparison | Result | Certainty | Evidence base |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Nicotine e-cigarettes vs NRT | RR 1.55 (1.28–1.88) | High | 9 studies, 2,703 participants |
| Nicotine vs non-nicotine e-cigarettes | RR 1.34 (1.06–1.70) | Moderate | 7 studies, 1,918 participants |
| E-cigarettes vs behavioural support or no support | RR 1.78 (1.42–2.25) | Low | 11 studies, 6,819 participants |
| Adverse events vs NRT | RR 1.00 (0.73–1.37) | Moderate | 7 studies, 2,241 participants |
| Serious adverse events vs NRT | RR 1.22 (0.73–2.03) | Low — insufficient | 8 studies, 2,950 participants |

The comparison against non-nicotine e-cigarettes matters because it separates the effect of nicotine from the effect of the device and the ritual. Nicotine-containing devices did better than placebo devices (RR 1.34), which suggests that nicotine is doing real work, not just the habit of holding something.

The largest single trial, Hajek and colleagues in 2019, randomised 886 adults attending UK stop-smoking services to e-cigarettes or NRT, both with behavioural support. One-year abstinence was 18.0% with e-cigarettes and 9.9% with NRT. That is a striking result for a single trial, and it comes with the caveat that both groups received support, and the products and setting were British.

## Why the guidance splits

The same evidence is read differently by different bodies, and the disagreement is about population policy as much as about the trials.

| Body | Position on e-cigarettes for quitting |
| --- | --- |
| WHO (2024) | Evidence insufficient to recommend e-cigarettes as cessation aids at population level; concerns about uptake among young people and dual use |
| USPSTF (2021, US) | I statement: evidence insufficient to assess benefits and harms for cessation; clinicians should direct patients to interventions with proven effectiveness and established safety |
| NICE NG209 (UK) | E-cigarettes can be considered as a stop-smoking aid, within local regulations |
| NHS (UK) | Likely far less harmful than cigarettes and can help you quit; not recommended for non-smokers; licensed NRT preferred in pregnancy |

The split is not a conspiracy and it is not stupidity. WHO is weighing population-level effects, including the possibility that a product which helps some adults quit also recruits young people who would never have smoked. NICE and the NHS are weighing what to offer an individual smoker sitting in a consultation. Those are different questions, and they can produce different answers from the same trial data.

For a reader, the practical consequence is this: e-cigarettes are a legitimate option in some health systems and a discouraged one in others. What you can access, and what the clinician in front of you recommends, will depend on where you live. If that conversation turns to medication, the [NRT versus varenicline comparison](/comparisons/nrt-vs-varenicline/) covers the two first-line pharmacological routes.

## The absolute numbers matter more than the ratio

A risk ratio of 1.55 sounds large. In absolute terms, the Cochrane review estimates about three extra quitters per 100 people. That is a real benefit, and it is smaller than the ratio suggests.

This is not a criticism of e-cigarettes. [NRT's own effect](/comparisons/nrt-vs-no-medication/) is also modest in absolute terms: most people who use it still do not quit on that attempt. The point is that no product does most of the work. The combination of medication and behavioural support does more than either alone — the case for [combination NRT](/comparisons/combination-nrt-vs-single-form/) rests on the same finding — and that result is consistent across the guidelines.

## What "using it properly" means

The single most important condition in the evidence is complete replacement. The trials that show a benefit randomised people to switch from cigarettes to e-cigarettes, not to add vaping on top of smoking.

NHS guidance is explicit: you will not get the full benefit unless you stop smoking cigarettes completely. That means no "just one" at the pub, no pack kept for emergencies, and no dual use disguised as cutting down. If you continue smoking, the exposure to tobacco smoke continues, and the reason to switch disappears.

The second condition is enough nicotine. Under-dosing is a common reason people conclude that vaping does not work: a device that delivers too little, used too cautiously, produces the irritation of quitting without the relief. The right strength is a conversation with a stop-smoking service or a specialist retailer, not a number from a website. This page does not give strengths or dosing, because those depend on the device, the liquid and the person.

The third condition is a device that works reliably. A leaking pod or a dying battery at the moment of a craving is a relapse risk, not an inconvenience. People who keep a spare device and a spare liquid are less likely to buy cigarettes in desperation.

## Safety: what the trials can tell you, and what they cannot

In the pooled trials, adverse events were similar between e-cigarettes and NRT (RR 1.00), and serious adverse events were rare in both groups. The most commonly reported problems were throat and mouth irritation, headache, cough and nausea, which tended to settle with continued use.

The trials cannot tell you about ten or twenty years of vaping, because they do not run that long and the products change faster than the evidence does. NHS guidance describes e-cigarettes as likely to be far less harmful than cigarettes because they do not produce tar or carbon monoxide, while stating that the long-term risks are not yet clear. That is the honest position: better than smoking, not established as harmless.

Two further cautions belong here. WHO notes that e-liquids vary in nicotine content, that some products claiming to be nicotine-free have been found to contain nicotine, and that illicit or unregulated products carry additional risks. In the United States, the EVALI outbreak of 2019 was linked mainly to vitamin E acetate in THC-containing products, not to regulated nicotine e-liquids — but it is a reminder that what is in the device matters.

## Pregnancy, young people and non-smokers

Pregnancy is the clearest case where the guidance does not split. NHS advises that licensed NRT products are the recommended option for pregnant people who smoke, and that the safety of e-cigarettes in pregnancy is not known. USPSTF finds the evidence insufficient for pharmacotherapy in pregnancy and recommends behavioural support. If you are pregnant, the conversation belongs with a clinician, and the [pregnancy page](/populations/pregnancy/) sets out the reasoning.

For people who do not smoke, the answer is simpler: do not start. E-cigarettes are not recommended for non-smokers, they are addictive, and the benefit in the trials applies to people replacing cigarettes, not to people adding nicotine. WHO's concern about uptake among adolescents is squarely about that population.

Age restrictions vary by country. In the UK, e-cigarettes cannot be sold to people under 18. If you are buying for a young person, that is not a cessation strategy; it is a regulatory offence and a health risk.

## The dual-use trap

Dual use is the most common way an e-cigarette attempt fails, and it is easy to slip into. Vaping is convenient indoors, so it replaces the cigarettes you can vape instead of; the ones that remain are the ones tied to work, driving or drinking. The result is a lower cigarette count and continued tobacco exposure.

The trials did not test that pattern. They tested switching. If you are vaping and still smoking, the useful question is not "how many have I cut down?" but "which cigarettes have I not replaced, and what would it take to replace them?" The [cravings guide](/cravings/) and the [trigger pages](/triggers/) are built around that question, the [withdrawal timeline](/withdrawal/timeline/) shows what the first weeks feel like, and the [relapse section](/relapse/) covers what to do when a dual-use pattern has already set in.

## Who might prefer which

| If this describes you | Worth discussing first | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| You have tried NRT and disliked the form or the routine | E-cigarettes, with support | The ritual and throat hit are part of what you are replacing |
| You want a licensed, standardised product | NRT | Consistent nicotine content and a long safety record |
| You are pregnant or breastfeeding | Licensed NRT under clinical guidance | E-cigarette safety in pregnancy is unknown |
| You want something discreet at work | Patch, lozenge or gum | No aerosol, no device |
| You have a strong hand-to-mouth habit | E-cigarettes or an inhalator | Both address the behavioural part, not just nicotine |
| You are not prepared to stop cigarettes completely | Neither is likely to deliver the trial result | Dual use preserves the exposure the switch is meant to remove |
| Cost is the binding constraint | Generic NRT, or a subsidised quitline supply | The cheapest option you will use consistently wins |
| You want the strongest guideline backing | [NRT](/medications/) or [varenicline](/medications/varenicline/) | Recommended first-line by WHO, USPSTF and NICE |

## Cost and access

NRT is widely available over the counter — [patches](/medications/nicotine-patch/), [gum](/medications/nicotine-gum/) and [lozenges](/medications/nicotine-lozenge/) are the common forms — and generic products are relatively cheap. Many national quitlines and health services supply it at reduced cost or free, sometimes with behavioural support attached.

E-cigarettes are a consumer purchase in most countries: no prescription, no clinical supervision, and a wide price range from disposable devices to refillable kits. The upfront cost of a refillable device can be higher than a week of NRT, though the running cost may be lower. The hidden cost is the trial-and-error of finding a device and liquid that work, which is where specialist advice saves money.

Access is not only about price. In countries where e-cigarettes are banned or tightly restricted, the comparison is academic. In countries where they are sold openly but not regulated as medicines, the quality question is real. The [medications section](/medications/) covers the licensed options in detail.

## A seven-day switch plan

| Day | Action | What to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Decide whether you are switching or cutting down | Switching is what the trials tested; cutting down is not |
| 2 | Choose a device or an NRT form, and buy enough for two weeks | Will you run out before the hardest week ends? |
| 3 | Set a quit date and remove cigarettes from the house, car and bag | Is there a pack within reach anywhere? |
| 4 | Use the product at the first craving, not after it peaks | Are you under-using out of caution? |
| 5 | Identify the cigarettes you have not replaced | Which situations still feel impossible without one? |
| 6 | Adjust technique or device with a specialist, not by guessing | Is the problem the product or the method? |
| 7 | Review with a stop-smoking service or pharmacist | Do you have a plan for the next trigger-heavy week? |

The plan is deliberately about switching, not tapering. Neither e-cigarettes nor NRT is a tapering schedule in the trials; the comparison is between replacing cigarettes and continuing them.

## What this page cannot tell you

It cannot tell you which device or liquid to buy, what nicotine strength to use, whether vaping is safe for your medical history, or whether it is legal where you live. Those are questions for a stop-smoking service, a pharmacist or a clinician. The evidence summarised here is population-level and current to the sources below, and it does not predict any individual outcome. This page has not been reviewed by a clinician.

The short version is uncomfortable but honest: e-cigarettes outperform NRT in trials, the effect is meaningful but modest in absolute terms, the guidance genuinely disagrees, and the benefit depends on stopping cigarettes rather than adding a device. If you want the most established safety record and a supervised course, NRT remains the more conservative choice. If you have failed NRT and are prepared to switch completely, e-cigarettes are a defensible option in countries whose guidance permits them — with support, not instead of it.
