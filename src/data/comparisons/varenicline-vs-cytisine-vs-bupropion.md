Three prescription medicines sit at the centre of smoking cessation treatment, and they do not work the same way. Two are partial agonists that occupy the nicotine receptor; one is an antidepressant that never touches it. The choice between them is usually made on four things: how strong the evidence is, which side effects a person can tolerate, what is available, and what it costs.

This page compares the three at the level of class evidence. It does not recommend a medicine for any individual, does not give doses or schedules, and cannot replace a prescriber who knows your medical history.

## Two mechanisms behind three medicines

**[Varenicline](/medications/varenicline/)** was developed from cytisine and acts as a partial agonist at the nicotinic receptor. It occupies the receptor and produces a moderate dopamine response — enough to take the edge off withdrawal, not enough to reproduce the reward of a cigarette. If a person smokes while taking it, the cigarette is blunted. That combination of easing withdrawal and reducing reward is why it is classed separately from nicotine replacement.

**[Cytisine](/medications/cytisine/)** is the older molecule in the same family. It is plant-derived and has been used for smoking cessation in Central and Eastern Europe for decades. Its receptor action is similar to varenicline's, and it is considerably cheaper in the markets where it is sold. The practical difference is not mechanism but evidence base, availability and the length of a standard course.

**[Bupropion](/medications/bupropion/)** is a different animal. It was developed as an antidepressant and later licensed for smoking cessation. It inhibits the reuptake of dopamine and noradrenaline, which is thought to reduce withdrawal and craving without acting on nicotine receptors at all. It is started before the quit date so that levels are established by the time cigarettes stop.

The mechanism matters because it predicts the side-effect profile, and because it explains why a switch between classes can change tolerability even when the quit plan stays the same. The [withdrawal timeline](/withdrawal/timeline/) shows what each class is being asked to cover. Partial agonists tend to cause nausea and sleep effects, because they act where nicotine acts. Bupropion tends to cause dry mouth and insomnia, and carries a seizure risk that has nothing to do with nicotine.

## What the trial evidence shows

The strongest single trial in this field is EAGLES, which randomised 8,144 smokers — half of them with a psychiatric diagnosis — to varenicline, bupropion, nicotine patch or placebo, all with brief counselling. For abstinence in weeks 9 to 12, varenicline beat placebo (odds ratio 3.61), nicotine patch (1.68) and bupropion (1.75). Bupropion and the patch were both better than placebo, at odds ratios of 2.07 and 2.15.

EAGLES is also the trial that reset the safety conversation. Moderate-to-severe neuropsychiatric events were not significantly more common on varenicline or bupropion than on patch or placebo, in either the psychiatric or the non-psychiatric cohort. That finding led regulators to remove the boxed neuropsychiatric warning from varenicline. It did not make the drugs risk-free; it made the earlier signal look smaller than feared.

Cytisine's evidence comes from smaller but consistent trials. In a placebo-controlled trial of 740 smokers, sustained abstinence at 12 months was 8.4% with cytisine versus 2.4% with placebo. In a later trial of 1,310 smokers that compared cytisine with nicotine replacement, one-month abstinence was 40% versus 31%, and the advantage persisted at six months — although self-reported adverse events, mainly nausea, vomiting and sleep problems, were more frequent on cytisine.

The direct comparison that people most want is cytisine against varenicline. It exists: 1,452 smokers in Australia, randomised to a standard course of one or the other, with telephone support offered to both. Verified six-month abstinence was 11.7% on cytisine and 13.3% on varenicline. The trial did not meet its prespecified non-inferiority margin, so the honest reading is that cytisine did not demonstrate equivalence — not that it was clearly worse. Self-reported adverse events were less frequent on cytisine.

| Comparison | What the trial found | How to read it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Varenicline vs placebo | OR 3.61 for weeks 9–12 abstinence (EAGLES, n=8,144) | Large, well-controlled, short endpoint |
| Varenicline vs patch | OR 1.68 (EAGLES) | Direct comparison within one trial |
| Varenicline vs bupropion | OR 1.75 (EAGLES) | Direct comparison within one trial |
| Bupropion vs placebo | OR 2.07 (EAGLES); roughly doubles in Cochrane review | Consistent across sources |
| Cytisine vs placebo | 8.4% vs 2.4% sustained 12-month abstinence (n=740) | Single-centre trial |
| Cytisine vs NRT | 40% vs 31% at one month; advantage persisted at six months (n=1,310) | Open-label, self-reported primary outcome |
| Cytisine vs varenicline | 11.7% vs 13.3% at six months; non-inferiority not shown (n=1,452) | Open-label; absolute rates low in both arms |

Two cautions apply to the whole table. First, trial abstinence rates are group averages from people who volunteered for a trial, often with counselling attached; they are not a prediction for any individual. Second, "not significantly different" is not the same as "the same". The cytisine-versus-varenicline trial is a good example: the point estimate was close, but the confidence interval did not exclude a meaningful advantage for varenicline.

## Side effects and safety

**Varenicline** most commonly causes nausea, which affected about a quarter of participants in EAGLES and is usually mild and early. Insomnia, abnormal dreams and headache are also common. The neuropsychiatric signal that once dominated headlines was not reproduced in EAGLES, but mood and behaviour changes should still be reported to the prescriber promptly, and the drug needs individual assessment in significant kidney impairment — a decision for the prescriber.

**Cytisine** causes nausea, vomiting, sleep disturbance and headache. In the comparison with nicotine replacement, gastrointestinal upset was the most frequent reason for stopping. It has a short course, which some people find easier and others find abrupt. Availability and regulatory status differ sharply between countries: it is not FDA-approved in the United States, which is why USPSTF does not cover it, while WHO recommends it.

**Bupropion** causes dry mouth, insomnia and headache. The serious concern is seizure risk, which is why it is contraindicated in people with a seizure disorder, a current or past eating disorder, or recent use of an MAOI antidepressant. It can also cause mood changes and should not be combined with certain other antidepressants without specialist review. These are prescribing decisions, not self-management decisions.

| Side effect | Varenicline | Cytisine | Bupropion |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Nausea | Common, usually early and mild | Common | Uncommon |
| Sleep problems | Insomnia, abnormal dreams | Sleep disturbance | Insomnia |
| Mouth or stomach | Dry mouth, constipation | Vomiting, abdominal upset | Dry mouth |
| Serious concern | Mood changes; kidney impairment | GI intolerance; limited long-term data | Seizure risk; mood changes |

None of these medicines is safe for everyone, and none is free of interactions. A prescriber needs the full medication list, including anything bought over the counter, before choosing.

## Cost, access and regulatory status

Cost is not a footnote in this comparison; it decides which option many people can actually use. Varenicline is prescription-only in most markets. Generic versions have reduced the price in some countries, but it remains the most expensive of the three in many settings.

Cytisine is the opposite case: it is cheap, and in some countries it is sold in pharmacies without a prescription. That affordability is part of why WHO recommends it for adults, and why it is attractive in health systems where varenicline is unaffordable. The catch is uneven availability and regulation — a medicine that is first-line in one country may be unavailable in the next.

Bupropion is generic and usually inexpensive, but it is prescription-only and not licensed for cessation everywhere. Its cost advantage is smaller than cytisine's and its evidence base is older than varenicline's.

Guideline positions reflect those differences rather than a single ranking. WHO (2024) recommends varenicline, NRT, bupropion and cytisine. USPSTF (2021) recommends varenicline and bupropion, and does not cover cytisine because it is not FDA-approved. NICE NG209 recommends varenicline, combination NRT and bupropion. The [medications pillar](/medications/) sets out the full comparison, including NRT.

## Who each option might suit

| If this describes you | Worth discussing | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| You want the single medicine with the strongest evidence | Varenicline | Largest trial base; direct wins over patch and bupropion |
| Cost is the deciding constraint and cytisine is available | Cytisine | Much cheaper; effective versus placebo and NRT |
| You had troublesome nausea on a partial agonist | Bupropion | Different mechanism, different side-effect profile |
| You have a seizure disorder or a history of an eating disorder | Not bupropion | Contraindicated; discuss a partial agonist or NRT |
| You have significant kidney impairment | Prescriber review first | Varenicline and cytisine both need individual assessment |
| You are pregnant or breastfeeding | Behavioural support first | USPSTF finds insufficient evidence for medication; any use is a clinician decision |
| You have a psychiatric diagnosis | Any of the three, with monitoring | EAGLES found no significant excess of neuropsychiatric events |
| You tried one and relapsed | A different class, with support | Switching is reasonable; repeating the same plan is less so |

This table is a conversation starter, not a prescribing rule. Two people with the same row can still need different answers because of interactions, kidney function, pregnancy, mental health history or previous reactions.

## Medication plus behavioural support

Every major guideline pairs medication with behavioural support, and the combination outperforms either alone. That support can be a quitline, a structured counselling programme, a digital programme or a clinician follow-up. The [counselling page](/methods/counseling/) explains what the evidence covers, and the [medication versus behavioural support comparison](/comparisons/medication-vs-behavioral-support/) sets out why the question is usually "both", not "either".

Combination NRT is the other pharmacological route worth knowing about. Network meta-analyses place it alongside varenicline among the most effective options, and it may be preferable where a nicotine-receptor drug is unsuitable. The [combination NRT comparison](/comparisons/combination-nrt-vs-single-form/) covers that arrangement, and the [NRT versus no medication comparison](/comparisons/nrt-vs-no-medication/) puts the size of the class effect in context.

## Questions worth asking before you choose

- Is there anything in my medical history, kidney function or other medicines that makes one of these unsuitable?
- What side effects should I expect, and which ones mean I should contact you rather than stop?
- How long is the course, and what happens if I miss days or stop early?
- If I smoke while taking it, what should I do?
- What support is available alongside the medicine, and is it included in the cost?
- If this one does not suit me, what is the next option?

These are the questions a prescriber can answer with your history in front of them. A website cannot. If you are unsure whether you need a prescription at all, the question on [whether you need a doctor to quit smoking](/questions/do-i-need-a-doctor-to-quit-smoking/) separates self-directed attempts from ones that need clinical support.

## What this page cannot tell you

It cannot tell you which medicine to take, what dose, how to taper, or whether any of them is safe for your particular combination of conditions. It reports group-level trial results and guideline positions, current to the sources below. It has not been reviewed by a clinician, and it is information rather than medical advice.

The practical takeaway is narrower than a ranking: varenicline has the best evidence among single medications, cytisine is a credible lower-cost alternative where it is available, and bupropion remains a useful option when partial agonists do not fit. The best choice is the one a prescriber can justify for you — and the one you can stay on long enough, with support, to give it a fair chance.
